# User Personas

## Persona 1: Maria — The Primary Caregiver

### Demographics
- Mother, late 30s–40s
- Has a teen (13–17) showing signs of a mood disorder
- May have her own neurodivergent traits (ADHD mentioned as a design consideration)
- Christian faith is part of her worldview and coping framework
- Technically capable but not a developer — comfortable with apps, frustrated by bad UX

### Situation
Maria's teen has been showing increasingly concerning behavioral patterns — explosive rage one week, can't-get-out-of-bed withdrawal the next. School is calling. The pediatrician said "all teens are moody." Maria knows this is different but can't articulate *why* in a 15-minute appointment.

She may or may not have a formal diagnosis yet. If she does, it's likely bipolar disorder (or suspected bipolar), and she's tracking to help her clinician adjust treatment. If she doesn't, she's gathering evidence to get taken seriously.

### Goals
1. **Prove she's not imagining it.** She needs data that shows patterns over time, not just anecdotes.
2. **Prepare for clinician appointments.** Walk in with a structured report, not a jumbled narrative.
3. **Understand what's coming.** Predict difficult periods so she can prepare, not just react.
4. **Survive the daily grind.** Log quickly on hard days. Get encouragement. Remember the good days exist.
5. **Feel supported, not judged.** The app should feel like a trusted tool, not a clinical checklist.

### Behaviors
- Logs most often in the evening after the teen is in bed
- On hard days, uses Quick Log only (mood + day quality) — too drained for detail
- On calmer days, fills in behaviors, impairments, and notes
- Checks the dashboard for patterns before appointments
- Exports PDF reports for the psychiatrist
- Wants to see the wave graph to understand cycles
- Values the AI journal import because she can vent in her own words and the app structures it

### Pain Points
- Exhausted. Any friction in the app is a reason to skip logging.
- Has been gaslit by systems. Needs the app to take her observations seriously.
- Forgets to log. Needs non-annoying reminders that feel personal, not robotic.
- Overwhelmed by too many options. The behavior checklist must be scannable, not a wall of text.
- Doesn't want to see the same notification twice. Novelty matters (especially if she's ND herself).

### Quote
> "I need someone — or something — to tell me I'm not crazy. And I need my psychiatrist to see what I see."

## Persona 2: Dr. Reeves — The Clinician

### Demographics
- Child/adolescent psychiatrist or psychologist
- Sees 15–25 patients per day
- Limited time per appointment (15–30 minutes)
- Familiar with DSM-5 criteria but doesn't have time to teach parents how to observe

### Situation
Dr. Reeves has a patient whose parent says "he's been really bad lately." That's not actionable. She needs structured, dated observations mapped to diagnostic criteria. She wants to see:
- How many days in the past month met manic vs. depressive thresholds
- Whether episodes meet DSM-5 duration criteria
- What the medication adherence looks like
- Whether there are safety concerns

### Goals
1. **Get structured data.** Frequency counts, severity ratings, and timeline — not narratives.
2. **See it fast.** A one-page summary she can scan in 2 minutes before the appointment.
3. **Trust the framework.** The mapping to DSM-5 criteria must be accurate and transparent.
4. **Recommend the tool.** If it's good, she'll tell other parents to "use this for a few months before your next appointment."

### Behaviors
- Receives a PDF export from the caregiver (does not use the app directly)
- Scans the wave graph for cycle patterns
- Checks episode detection for DSM-5 duration match
- Looks at medication adherence (missed med days)
- Reviews caregiver notes for qualitative context

### Pain Points
- No time to learn a new tool. The report must be self-explanatory.
- Skeptical of parent-reported data unless it's structured and consistent.
- Concerned about apps that overstate clinical certainty — "possible episode" is fine, "diagnosed manic episode" is not.

### Quote
> "If a parent walks in with three months of daily observations mapped to DSM-5 criteria, that changes the entire appointment."

## Persona 3: Jake — The Teen Self-Tracker

### Demographics
- 15–17 years old
- Diagnosed or suspected bipolar disorder
- Has a smartphone (iPhone)
- Aesthetic preferences matter — he won't use an app that looks clinical or "for old people"

### Situation
Jake's mom invited him to his own project in Storm Tracker. He logs his own mood and behaviors. His entries are tagged as self-observations and displayed alongside his mom's caregiver observations. Discrepancies between their logs are flagged — and those discrepancies are clinically valuable.

### Goals
1. **Feel ownership.** This is *his* tracking, not something being done *to* him.
2. **Express himself.** The app should feel personal — theming, customization, his aesthetic.
3. **Understand himself.** See his own patterns without clinical jargon.

### Behaviors
- Logs sporadically — some days he's engaged, some days he won't touch it
- Prefers the app to feel like a personal journal, not a medical form
- Responds well to encouraging notifications on good days
- Ignores notifications that feel naggy
- Wants the app to look cool (anime theme, bold colors)

### Pain Points
- Doesn't want to feel monitored or surveilled
- Resistant to anything that feels like homework
- Privacy matters — he may not want his mom to see everything

### Quote
> "I'll use it if it doesn't feel like a doctor's office on my phone."

## Persona 4: Sarah — The Co-Caregiver

### Demographics
- Stepparent, grandparent, or co-parent
- Invited to the project by the primary caregiver
- Observes the teen in different contexts (e.g., other household, weekends)

### Situation
Sarah sees the teen every other weekend. She notices different behaviors than Maria does — the teen might be more relaxed at her house, or more explosive. Her observations provide a second data point. Discrepancies between her logs and Maria's are valuable, not contradictory.

### Goals
1. **Contribute observations** without duplicating Maria's work.
2. **See the big picture** that Maria is building.
3. **Feel included** in the care team.

### Behaviors
- Logs less frequently (only during her custody time)
- Reads the dashboard to understand what's been happening between visits
- Does not export reports — that's Maria's role

### Pain Points
- Unclear what she should be logging vs. what Maria already covered
- Doesn't want to undermine Maria's observations
- May have a different relationship with the teen and see different behaviors

### Quote
> "I want to help, but I don't want to step on Maria's toes."
